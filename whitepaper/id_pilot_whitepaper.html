Predicting SNAP Payment Errors:
A Pilot Proposal for Idaho

Synthetic-data-validated case prioritization for quality-control review — tested where errors are rare
October 4, 2026  ·  Research whitepaper  ·  Methods and validation artifacts are open-source at github.com/ctrimm/snap-qc-per-experiments

1. Executive summary

Idaho has the second-lowest SNAP payment error rate in the nation (3.85% FY2025, federally published). That makes it a hard and valuable test of our approach: if synthetic-data-trained models can find errors where errors are rare, the method is robust. We built and validated an Idaho-conditioned synthetic data generator, calibrated against federal quality-control (QC) microdata (3,839 Idaho cases, FY2020–2024; generator fitted on FY2022–2024). Machine-learning models trained only on this synthetic data predict the type of real, held-out Idaho errors better than a nationally trained model — and the edge survived a fully clean refit that excluded every holdout row. On the error-vs-clean ranking task the Idaho-conditioned model ties the national model; the transferable signal lives in the error-type task. Proof the synthetic cases carry genuine signal about where Idaho's errors happen, even in one of the nation's cleanest caseloads.

The operational finding — and the track's key scientific result: ranking cases by error probability alone is the winning rule, recovering $3,000 vs $2,143 for random review at a fixed review budget — $31 vs $22 per hour of reviewer time. This replicates the South Dakota reversal and contradicts the Pennsylvania/New York pattern, where probability-alone was worse than random and dollar-aware rankers won. The emerging rule: where errors are rare and small, rank by probability; where a big-dollar tail exists, model the dollars. Reviewing the riskiest half of cases catches 68% of all errors with half the reviews.

We propose a low-risk pilot: train the ranker on Idaho's own historical QC data, validate on a recent holdout, then run it in shadow alongside existing review. No production eligibility-system changes. No new data collection.

2. The problem

SNAP payment error rates remain stubbornly high nationwide, and every error is either taxpayer money over-issued or a household underpaid. Quality-control review is the corrective mechanism — but reviewer time is finite, and cases are currently selected for review without a data-driven sense of where errors concentrate. The result is expensive review effort spread thinly over a caseload where risk is highly uneven.

Idaho is a demanding test of a targeted approach. Our analysis of 3,839 Idaho QC cases (FY2020–2024) shows the state's benefit-calculation engine is clean (the statutory formula predicts issued benefits exactly on 93.4–97.2% of cases per year, within a point of the national fleet; no uncapped-shelter anomaly) — Idaho's errors live in the human process layer (verification, timing, documentation), which is precisely where a prediction tool adds value. The error signature is distinctive: timing errors — cases the agency was not yet required to act on — run at 1.57x the national rate and make up 62% of all variances; shelter-deduction errors run 1.40x and dominate the caseload; the state codes client-caused errors at 1.66x the national rate. Error dollars are small (median $15 vs $28 nationally; 90th percentile $64 vs $186) and hyper-concentrated: the top tenth of errors by dollar size holds 74% of all error dollars.

3. What was built and validated

An Idaho-conditioned synthetic generator. Starting from a nationally calibrated generator, we fit Idaho-specific error-rate offsets and empirical error mixes on FY2022–2024 microdata (FY2020–21 excluded as partial-year files). The Idaho-conditioned output reproduces the state's finding mix (L1 0.0215), element mix (L1 0.083), and cause mix (L1 0.096), while leaving national output byte-identical. Documented structural residuals: the dollar median undershoots ($7 vs $19 — the relative-offset architecture cannot express the timing-vs-substantive dollar shape) and the rare guarded buckets (dependent care, household composition, arithmetic) draw at national rates.

Validated predictive signal — with an honest qualification. Models trained only on Idaho-conditioned synthetic cases beat the nationally trained model at predicting which type of error a held-out Idaho case has (element accuracy wins on 5 of 6 seed comparisons; the accuracy edge persists under a fully clean refit that excluded all 960 holdout rows: +5.4pp over national). On the error-vs-clean ranking task, Idaho-conditioning ties the national model (finding AUC +0.017 pristine, within noise). The transferable signal is real, but it lives in the error-type and dollar tasks, not in fine-grained error-vs-clean ranking — the mirror image of the New York result, and the fourth state to confirm there is no universal conditioning story.

Probability-alone wins — the South Dakota replication. In Pennsylvania and New York, ranking by error probability alone was worse than random at the top slice, because probability and error size were nearly orthogonal: the dollars lived in a big-dollar income tail that probability couldn't see. Idaho, like South Dakota, has no big-dollar tail (90th percentile: $87 vs $70 in SD), and the synthetic dollar regressors are miscalibrated — multiplying a good probability ranker (AUC 0.68) by a bad dollar estimate hurts: the two-part rule recovers less than random at the top-10% budget (0.61x). Probability-alone is the best rule at the top-10% and top-30% budgets and ties at top-20%. Two low-error, small-dollar states now show this reversal against two higher-error states — the ranking rule must be re-fit to each state's error economics, not copied between states.

Headline pilot economics (simulated on held-out Idaho cases, top-20% review budget):

Savings-only value: $24.97/hr vs ~$13.4/hr for random review. Absolute dollars are small — Idaho's errors are rare and small — but the lift over random (1.40x) is real, and the probability-only rule is also the fairest configuration tested (see below).

4. The pilot

What Idaho provides

  • Historical QC case extracts the state already holds (no new data collection, no new PII).
  • A review team willing to work a ranked queue for a defined pilot period.

What changes — and what does not

  • Phase 0 (cold start, no Idaho data needed): the synthetic-trained ranker scores a sample of cases; rankings are validated against known outcomes.
  • Phase 1: the ranker is trained on Idaho's historical QC data and validated on a recent holdout the model never saw. Phase 1 also selects the ranking rule for Idaho's economics (probability-alone vs dollar-aware) rather than inheriting another state's.
  • Phase 2 (shadow): the ranker scores the live review caseload; reviewers work the top slice; outcomes are measured against the baseline selection method.
  • What does not change: no modifications to the eligibility system, no automated determinations (the model prioritizes review — it never decides a case), no data leaves the state's control.

Success metrics

  • Precision in the reviewed slice vs baseline selection.
  • Error dollars recovered per reviewer hour vs baseline.
  • Reviewer time saved to reach a fixed error-capture target.

A reasonable pilot shape is 8–12 weeks from data handoff to shadow-readout.

5. Limitations and honest caveats

  • Mixed transfer — stated plainly. Idaho-conditioning improves error-type prediction (accuracy edge +5.4pp pristine) but does not beat the national model on error-vs-clean ranking (AUC +0.017, within noise). The pilot's value proposition rests on the ranking economics (validated below), not on a finding-task advantage.
  • Modest top-20% lift. The winning rule's 1.40x lift at the headline budget is below our ~1.5x bar, and it ties the direct dollar model there (1.40x vs 1.42x); the "probability-alone wins" claim rests on the top-10% (1.77x) and top-30% (1.26x) budgets plus simplicity. The holdout is 960 cases; the top-10% slice is 96 cases — confidence intervals are wide.
  • The ranking rule is state-specific. Probability-alone wins in Idaho because there is no big-dollar tail and the dollar regressors are miscalibrated. If Idaho's error economics shift (a big-dollar tail emerging), the rule must be re-tuned — Phase 1 selects the rule per state rather than assuming transfer.
  • Refit on full years, not annually. The transfer signal is year-to-year stable and the pooled three-year fit beats a single-year refit — but shelter-deduction errors showed real decay across the period boundary and should be watched, and FY2024 brought a coding-practice change (the state began recording causes on timing errors far more often; the cause-blank rate fell from 67% to 27%). Partial-year files (FY2020–21) are excluded from refits.
  • Procedural errors are invisible. The federally published payment error rate (3.85% FY2025) and the case-microdata-implied case-error rate (33.9%) measure different things: the published rate is dollar-weighted and includes procedural errors the microdata cannot see. This tool targets substantive case errors, not procedural compliance.
  • Headroom. The synthetic-trained ranker captures roughly two-thirds of the performance a model trained on real Idaho data is projected to reach (1.40x vs 2.08x lift). The pilot's Phase 1 exists to close exactly this gap.
  • Human judgment stays in the loop. This is a triage tool for reviewers, not an adjudication engine. Final determinations remain human — by design.

Review-burden note

In the Pennsylvania and New York tracks, dollar-maximizing rankers proved risk-regressive: they deprioritized elderly and disabled households (the highest-error groups) because their errors are frequent but small-dollar. We re-ran the household-type burden analysis on Idaho's probability-only winner: it is roughly twice as proportional to error risk (CV 0.29 vs ~0.59), reviews elderly/disabled households at 0.54–0.57x their risk (vs 0.22–0.35 for the dollar rankers), and does not over-review childless adults (0.40x vs 1.41–1.45x). The regressivity is a property of the dollar objective, not of ranking per se — and it is one more reason the ranking rule must be selected per state. A residual tilt remains and should be disclosed to the pilot partner.
