Predicting SNAP Payment Errors:
A Pilot Proposal for Pennsylvania

Synthetic-data-validated case prioritization for quality-control review
September 29, 2026  ·  Research whitepaper  ·  Methods and validation artifacts are open-source at github.com/ctrimm/snap-qc-per-experiments

1. Executive summary

We have built and validated a synthetic data generator that reproduces how SNAP payment errors occur in Pennsylvania's caseload, calibrated against five years of federal quality-control (QC) microdata. Machine-learning models trained only on this synthetic data predict real Pennsylvania errors meaningfully better than baseline — proof the synthetic cases carry genuine signal about where errors happen.

The operational finding: a two-part ranking model (probability of error × predicted error dollars), applied to prioritize QC review, recovers $6,074 vs $3,851 for random review at a fixed review budget — $75 vs $47 per hour of reviewer time. Reviewing the riskiest half of cases catches 67% of all errors with half the reviews.

We propose a low-risk pilot: train the ranker on Pennsylvania's own historical QC data, validate on a recent holdout, then run it in shadow alongside existing review. No production eligibility-system changes. No new data collection.

2. The problem

SNAP payment error rates remain stubbornly high nationwide, and every error is either taxpayer money over-issued or a household underpaid. Quality-control review is the corrective mechanism — but reviewer time is finite, and cases are currently selected for review without a data-driven sense of where errors concentrate. The result is expensive review effort spread thinly over a caseload where risk is highly uneven.

Pennsylvania is a strong candidate for a targeted approach. Our analysis of 3,268 Pennsylvania QC cases (FY2020–2024) shows the state's benefit-calculation engine matches federal statute essentially exactly (99.7–100% of cases) — Pennsylvania's errors live in the human process layer (verification, timing, documentation), which is precisely where a prediction tool adds value. Error patterns are also concentrated: shelter-deduction errors run at roughly twice the national rate, while computation errors run at a tenth.

3. What was built and validated

A Pennsylvania-conditioned synthetic generator. Starting from a nationally calibrated generator (error-type mix matching reality within ~1 percentage point), we fit Pennsylvania-specific error-rate offsets and empirical error mixes. The PA-conditioned output reproduces Pennsylvania's finding mix, element mix, and cause mix within a few percentage points, while leaving national output unchanged.

Validated predictive signal. Models trained only on PA-conditioned synthetic cases predict real, held-out Pennsylvania errors: 0.556 element-level accuracy vs a 0.453 baseline, and PA-conditioning genuinely beats a nationally trained model. The one honest threat to this result — whether the generator had indirectly seen the holdout data — was tested with a fully clean refit; the advantage survived.

A dollar-aware ranking. The set's most important surprise: ranking cases by error probability alone performs worse than random at the top slice. Error probability and error size are nearly orthogonal in Pennsylvania's mix — the dollars live in income errors, not the frequent shelter errors. The product must therefore be a two-part ranker: calibrated probability of error multiplied by predicted error dollars.

Headline pilot economics (simulated on held-out PA cases, top-20% review budget):

Error dollars recovered$3,851$6,074

Of the $6,074: $4,239 is over-issuance (direct program savings); $1,835 is under-issuance (households underpaid — the correct QC outcome, but a make-whole cost). Savings-only value: $52/hr vs $47/hr.

4. The pilot

What Pennsylvania provides

  • Historical QC case extracts the state already holds (no new data collection, no new PII).
  • A review team willing to work a ranked queue for a defined pilot period.

What changes — and what does not

  • Phase 0 (cold start, no PA data needed): the synthetic-trained ranker scores a sample of cases; rankings are validated against known outcomes.
  • Phase 1: the two-part ranker is trained on PA's historical QC data and validated on a recent holdout the model never saw.
  • Phase 2 (shadow): the ranker scores the live review caseload; reviewers work the top slice; outcomes are measured against the baseline selection method.
  • What does not change: no modifications to the eligibility system, no automated determinations (the model prioritizes review — it never decides a case), no data leaves the state's control.

Success metrics

  • Precision in the reviewed slice vs baseline selection.
  • Error dollars recovered per reviewer hour vs baseline.
  • Reviewer time saved to reach a fixed error-capture target.

A reasonable pilot shape is 8–12 weeks from data handoff to shadow-readout.

5. Limitations and honest caveats

  • Headroom. The synthetic-trained ranker captures roughly one-third of the performance a model trained on real PA data is projected to reach. The pilot's Phase 1 exists to close exactly this gap.
  • Small-dollar skew. The errors the ranker catches skew toward smaller dollar amounts; large-dollar errors remain harder to find and are the priority for Phase 1 feature work.
  • Procedural errors are invisible. Published federal error rates run 2–3 points above what case microdata implies, entirely in procedural errors the microdata cannot see. This tool targets substantive case errors, not procedural compliance.
  • Stability. The signal is year-to-year stable in our tests, but the error-probability baseline should be re-tuned on recent data at pilot start.
  • Human judgment stays in the loop. This is a triage tool for reviewers, not an adjudication engine. Final determinations remain human — by design.
